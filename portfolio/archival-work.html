Following the
Evidence

Recovering the stories of early women golf coaches and professionals.

Through archival research, I traced the lives and careers of pioneering women in golf, exploring how family relationships and sisterhood shaped their experiences of playing and teaching the game.

Focus Women's sporting history
Methods Archival & desk research
Sources Historical records & press
Output Published article
01

The question

Whose stories have been overlooked?

Women played an important role in the development of golf as both a competitive sport and a professional activity. Yet the lives of early women instructors and professionals have received comparatively limited attention.

I wanted to look beyond the familiar stories of elite players and explore the women who taught the game.

The article focused particularly on the relationships between sisters, asking how family connections may have provided opportunities for competition, learning and belonging.

02

The research

Piecing together lives from the archive.

I worked across a range of historical sources to reconstruct individual biographies and investigate their professional and family relationships.

01

Tracing biographies

Used birth, marriage and census records to establish identities, family connections and key details of individual lives.

02

Exploring contemporary accounts

Examined historical newspaper coverage and sporting publications to understand how women’s golfing careers were described at the time.

03

Connecting individual stories

Compared evidence across biographies to identify patterns in family relationships and professional golfing activity.

03

Working with evidence

When the records tell a different story.

Archival research often involves working with incomplete or conflicting information. One example emerged while investigating the identity of Blanch Anderson and her relationship to the golf professional Maud Gordon Robertson.

By examining birth and census records alongside existing historical accounts, I challenged an interpretation that Blanch Anderson may have been a name used by Maud. The evidence instead indicated that Blanch was Maud's younger sister.

This raised the possibility that some golfing activities previously attributed to Maud may have belonged to Blanch. It demonstrated how revisiting source material can complicate established narratives and bring overlooked individuals into view.

Historical identities are not always straightforward. Following the evidence can reveal a more complicated and more interesting story.
04

The findings

Seeing the wider picture through individual lives.

The research brought together the stories of May Hezlet, Maud Gordon Robertson, Lily Freemantle and D.M. Smyth. Their experiences revealed different routes into professional golf, from instruction and writing to club appointments.

A recurring pattern was the presence of sisters within golfing families. These relationships offered a way to explore how women practised and competed together, while developing their own expertise in a sport where men often held positions of influence.

Rather than treating these women as isolated pioneers, I used their biographies to explore the networks and relationships that supported women's involvement in the game.

Research in practice

From fragmented records to a compelling historical narrative.

This project demonstrates my ability to locate and interpret archival material, assess evidence across different sources, investigate historical identities and develop original research into an accessible article.

I particularly enjoy the investigative process: following a lead, scrutinising established accounts and discovering what the records can reveal about lives that might otherwise remain overlooked.
